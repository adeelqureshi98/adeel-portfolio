The Shifting World Order — and Where Pakistan Stands

By Muhammad Adeel
Original column written for this portfolio — selected writings

For most of the last three decades, the world ran on a simple script: one dominant power set the rules of trade, finance and security, and everyone else adjusted. That script is being rewritten in front of us. New economic blocs are forming, supply chains are being redrawn, and middle powers are discovering that they have more room to maneuver than they once believed.

This is not an abstract debate for think tanks. For a country like Pakistan — positioned at the crossroads of South Asia, Central Asia and the Middle East, with one of the world's youngest populations — the shifting order is a direct question about jobs, prices and the future.

What is actually changing

Three shifts matter most. First, the global economy is fragmenting into competing corridors and partnerships; trade now follows trust as much as price. Second, energy and food security have returned to the center of statecraft — countries that cannot feed and fuel themselves negotiate from weakness. Third, technology — from semiconductors to artificial intelligence — has become the new currency of power, and the race to control it is redrawing alliances.

In a multipolar world, geography is leverage — but only for countries that turn location into connectivity and connectivity into commerce.

Pakistan's real assets

Strip away the noise, and Pakistan holds genuine cards. Its location connects growing markets. Its diaspora is large, skilled and deeply linked to the homeland. Its IT and freelance sector has shown that Pakistani talent can compete globally from a laptop in Lahore or Kot Addu. And its young population is an asset — if it is educated, skilled and connected.

But assets are not outcomes. Location means little without ports, roads and policies that move goods quickly. A young population means little without classrooms that teach and industries that hire. The countries gaining from the new order share one trait: they converted their geography and demography into economic strategy, not just slogans.

The path worth taking

Pakistan's foreign and economic policy needs one compass: the citizen's kitchen table. Every alliance, every corridor, every trade deal should be judged by a single test — does it bring investment, jobs and cheaper energy to ordinary people? Ideological alignments come and go; economic partnerships built on mutual benefit endure.

The new world order will not wait for anyone's internal debates to settle. The window is open now — for export-led growth, for digital services, for regional trade. Windows, however, do not stay open forever. The nations that will matter in 2040 are making their moves in 2026.
